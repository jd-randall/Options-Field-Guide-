Options Field Guide

An option is a derivative: its value depends on an underlying asset. Every chart on this page responds to the sliders, so you can see how a position's profit changes when the stock moves, the strike changes or time passes. Prices are per share. One exchange-traded contract usually covers 100 shares.

Long is a right. Short is an obligation.

The buyer (holder) is long. They pay a premium and get a choice they can use or ignore. The seller (writer) is short. They collect the premium and must deliver if the buyer exercises. Pick one of the four positions below, then drag the stock price to see what happens at expiration.

CallPut Long buyer · holder Short seller · writer

Long positions risk only the premium paid. Short positions keep the premium at best, and must make good on the contract if exercised. Exchanges require writers to post margin for that reason, except for covered calls.

Strike (exercise) price, K
The price at which the stock can be bought or sold. Using that right is called exercising the option.
Premium
The price the holder pays the writer for the option.
Expiration date
When the contract ends. More time to expiration is worth more.
In / at / out of the money
In the money when exercising now would produce a positive cash flow: a call when S > K, a put when S < K. At the money when S = K.
American vs. European
American options can be exercised any time up to expiration; most US-listed options are American. European options only at expiration. The extra flexibility is the early exercise premium.
Standardized contracts
Exchange options fix the expiration dates, strikes and contract size (usually 100 shares). The Options Clearing Corporation stands between buyer and writer and guarantees performance.
Why trade options
Hedging softens the blow from volatility. Speculation places a bet. Arbitrage seeks a risk-free profit with zero net investment.
Dividends and splits
Cash dividends do not change the contract. An n-for-m split multiplies shares by n/m and the strike by m/n.

    Put-call parity and bounds

    Parity: a call plus cash worth K at expiration pays the same as a put plus the stock, so they must cost the same today. If any bound fails, there is an arbitrage.

    Valuation, the intuitive way

    One-step binomial tree

    Assume the stock can only go up or down. Buy just enough stock (Δ shares) and sell one option so that both outcomes pay the same. That payoff is risk-free, so discount it at the risk-free rate. Whatever is left over is the option's price. Defaults reproduce the textbook: $100 stock, up to $120 or down to $90, K = $110, r = 10%, one year → $6.19.

    Adding more steps to the tree makes the answer more accurate. With many small steps, the binomial price converges to Black-Scholes. Trees also handle American options and dividends, which Black-Scholes cannot.

    Worked examples

    Try each one first, then open it to see the solution. Some can be loaded into the calculators above.

    Protective putYou own Genco Oil at $45 and buy a put with a $30 strike for $5. The stock falls to $20. What are the total payoff and profit?Show solution
    Stock: 20 · Put: 30 − 20 = 10 · Payoff = $30 (= K)
    Stock loss: 20 − 45 = −25 · Option gain: 10 − 5 = +5
    Profit = −$20. The put floors the position at the $30 strike; without it the loss would be $25.
    Covered callYou own Genco Oil at $45 and sell a $45-strike call for $5. The stock stays at $45. What are the payoff and profit per share?Show solution
    Stock: 45 · Short call: 0 (not exercised) · Payoff = $45
    Stock gain: 45 − 45 = 0 · Option gain: 5 − 0 = +5
    Profit = +$5, the premium you kept.
    Black-ScholesS0 = $42, K = $40, six months, r = 10%, σ = 20%. What is the call premium?Show solution
    d₁ = [ln(42/40) + (0.10 + 0.5×0.20²)×0.5] / (0.20√0.5) = 0.7693
    d₂ = 0.7693 − 0.1414 = 0.6279
    C = 42×0.7791 − 38.05×0.7350 = 32.72 − 27.97 = $4.76
    Binomial treeA $100 stock will be $120 or $90 in a year. r = 10%. What is a one-year call with K = $110 worth?Show solution
    Δ = (10 − 0)/(120 − 90) = 1/3
    Risk-free payoff: 120×⅓ − 10 = 90×⅓ − 0 = 30
    PV = 30e−0.10 = 27.145 · C = 33.333 − 27.145 = $6.19
    Put-call parityS0 = $35, r = 10%, a six-month European put with K = $30 costs $2. What should the call cost?Show solution
    c = p + S₀ − Ke−rT
    = 2 + 35 − 30e−0.05 = 2 + 35 − 28.54 = $8.46
    Put-call parityS0 = $31, r = 10%, a three-month European call with K = $30 costs $3. What should the put cost?Show solution
    p = c − S₀ + Ke−rT
    = 3 − 31 + 30e−0.025 = 3 − 31 + 29.26 = $1.26
    Early exerciseA non-dividend stock is at $70. You hold an American call, K = $40, six months left, r = 10%. Should you exercise now?Show solution
    Exercise now: 70 − 40 = $30
    Premium ≥ S₀ − Ke−rT = 70 − 38.05 = $31.95
    No. Selling the call is worth at least $31.95. Exercising early throws away time value, so it is never optimal for a call on a non-dividend stock.
    ArbitrageA stock trades at $60 and a call on it is priced at $65. How do you lock in a risk-free profit?Show solution
    Buy the stock (−60), sell the call (+65): +5 today
    At expiration you hold the stock either way and deliver it at K if assigned, so you never owe more than you own. A call can never be worth more than the stock: c ≤ S₀.
    MarginA trader writes 4 call contracts, K = $40, stock at $38, premium $5. What is the margin requirement?Show solution
    Test 1: 400 × (5 + 0.20×38 − 2) = $4,240
    Test 2: 400 × (5 + 0.10×38) = $3,520
    Margin = $4,240, the greater of the two. The $2 is how far the call is out of the money.
    Assumption

    Volatility is known and fixed

    In reality it moves. The market's estimate is the implied volatility backed out of option prices (the VIX tracks it for the S&P 500), and it differs by strike, which is called the volatility smile. Buy options if you expect volatility to rise; sell them if you expect it to fall.

    Assumption

    The interest rate is fixed

    Rates change. Higher rates help calls (you pay the strike later) and hurt puts (you receive the strike later). Buy calls if you expect rates to rise, puts if you expect them to fall.

    Assumption

    No dividends

    Dividends drop the stock price, which hurts calls and helps puts. The dividend-adjusted model (the q slider above) uses S₀e−qT in place of S₀.

    Assumption

    European, frictionless, continuous

    Black-Scholes also assumes no early exercise, no taxes or trading costs, no price jumps and normally distributed returns. Binomial trees handle American options; Monte Carlo simulation handles exotic options.
